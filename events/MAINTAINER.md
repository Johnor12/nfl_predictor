# MAINTAINER

- __init__.py: `predictor(event, name=None)`: an implementation's `predict`, the event's `DEFAULT` one without a name. The scenarios' loaders all go through it.
- <event>/: one package per event (see its README.md and MAINTAINER.md).
- baseline_priors.py: `build` / `save` and the CLI (one season, or a range of them in parallel processes). One pass over a season's real plays, handing each one (a `shared.priors.Sample`) to every `<event>/predictors/baseline/priors.py`'s `observe(tally, sample)`; then each week's stat lines, added up by position group (`_totals`, a `shared.priors.Totals`), to every `observe_totals(tally, totals)`.
- situation.py: what is known before a scrimmage snap, as numbers: `features(ctx)` (the situation and the offense's game so far) and `personnel(group)`. The inputs the trained predictors share; each one's `inputs.py` adds its own.
- strength.py: who is playing, as numbers: `rates` and `total` (a team's, coach's, referee's, or player's past), `experience`, `position`, `quarterback`, `weather`. Shared by the trained predictors' `inputs.py` like `situation.py`.
- training.py: `table`: every real play of the given seasons as a row of a trained predictor's inputs and output, cached per season (`.cache/training/<name>_<season>.parquet`). `build`: several tables in one pass over the plays, the seasons in parallel processes. `trainers`: every `train.py`. `train_cli`: the command line every `train.py` shares, and this module's own (`python -m events.training`: all of them).
- test_strength.py: the rates of a team's form and of a player, the weather as numbers, the quarterback on the field.
- test_predictors.py: every baseline predictor together produces valid plays on synthetic contexts; a defender's own past moves his tackles, and the charted stats come out of simulated plays; `load_predictors` rejects unknown events; the baseline timeout and challenge predictors only name a team that may; fake kicks and the fair-catch kick play out with the default predictors.
- test_baseline_priors.py: a week 1 priors build covers every event that has a `priors.py` (19) with the committed files' keys (who is credited and the charted rates among them), for a season with on-field data (2025), one without (2026), and one in the older data formats (2016). Skipped if the nflverse cache is missing.

Adding an event: create `events/<event>/` with the output type in `__init__.py`, a `predictors/baseline/` exporting `predict`, and the READMEs; add a field to `scenarios.play.Predictors` and call it from `scenarios/play/chain.py`; add its cases to `evaluators/play/cases.py` so it gets scored.

Adding an event to another scenario works the same way: a field in that scenario's bundle (`scenarios.coin_toss.TossPredictors`, `scenarios.game.GamePredictors`) and a call in its chain. A baseline without data (`coin_toss`, `toss_choice`) has no `priors.py`.

Adding a predictor implementation: create `events/<event>/predictors/<name>/` exporting `predict`, then load it with `load_predictors(<event>="<name>")`. Score it with `python -m evaluators.play --swap <event>=<name>` and compare the event's rows with the default's; the audit shows the effect on overall rates. Once it measures better, make it the default: `DEFAULT = "<name>"` in `events/<event>/predictors/__init__.py` (the evaluators, the audit, and the simulators' CLIs then use it, and `--swap <event>=baseline` brings the old one back for a comparison).

Adding a trained predictor (a model instead of counts; `snap/predictors/gbm/` is the pattern for a yes/no output, `pass_result/predictors/gbm/` for one of several classes, `carry/predictors/gbm/` for a number): an `inputs.py` with one `features` function that reads the model's inputs the same way from a simulated play and a real one (`situation.features(ctx)` plus the event's own inputs as plain values, so `predict` can hand them over from the lineups and formations and `example` from the data), a `train.py` with `example(sample)` (the features and the real output of one real play), `TABLES` (its table's name -> `(example, columns)`, which is how `python -m events.training` finds it), `train(season)`, and a CLI (`training.train_cli`), and a `predict` that asks `shared.models.models_of(__file__)` for the season's model. Keep whatever the model doesn't predict in the baseline and call it (the baseline's `predict` takes the part to replace as an argument). Choose features and settings on seasons before the evaluated ones, so the evaluators' scores stay out of sample.

Notes:
- An event's `__init__.py` must not import `scenarios.play` (the play scenario imports it). Implementations may.
- In an implementation's `__init__.py`, don't give a module-level name the name of a sibling module: importing `priors.py` replaces a `priors` attribute on the package. That's why the loaders are called `load_priors`.
- A baseline's `priors.py` is only imported by `baseline_priors.py`; `predict` reads the built JSON.
- `python -m events.baseline_priors` rebuilds every event's file from the data as it is now, so a rebuild from an older cache than the committed files came from takes games out of them (check `git diff --stat` on the priors of an event you didn't touch). The current season's files refresh themselves (`shared/nflverse`), so that is a past season's, or a build made offline.
- After changing a `priors.py`, rebuild the priors of every season (`python -m events.baseline_priors`) and rerun `python -m evaluators.play` and `python -m evaluators.audit` to see the effect.
- What the data only counts per player and game (Pro Football Reference's charting: hurries, blitzes, missed and broken tackles, coverage, drops, bad throws, yards after contact) reaches a baseline as a league rate: its `priors.py` has an `observe_totals(tally, totals)` that turns a week's totals into yes/no counts (`carry`, `pass_rush`, `pass_result`). No play says who hurried the passer, so the rate is all the priors can hold, and the player comes from a pick by position group and his own past.
- Using everything a season's data has: a `priors.py` counts a key only on the samples that can say (`if personnel(sample): ...`), so the key lands in the seasons that have the data, and `predict` reads it like any other. A predictor input that only some seasons fill (`Player.snaps`) needs a fallback in `predict` for when it's empty.
- A key must mean the same thing in every season's file, because the seasons are summed. Where only some seasons can tell two outcomes apart, count the finer split under its own key on the samples that can (`pass_rush`'s `throwaway`, charted from 2022, next to `dropback`, which every season has). Counting it inside the shared key would water the rate down with the seasons that can't see it.
- A key whose event changed with a rule is tied to that rule where the predictor loads its priors: `priors_of(__file__, rules={"kickoff_distance": KICKOFF})` (`shared/rulebook`). So far: the kickoff keys of `free_kick`, `kick_result`, and `carry`, and the try keys of `kick_attempt` and `lineups`.
- A model can only use an input that an earlier event of the chain predicts as well as the model itself could: it is trained on the real value and handed a simulated one. The run-or-pass model got worse reading a shotgun drawn from down and distance, and no worse (and better when told the real one) once the formation came from a model that reads what it reads (`snap/predictors/gbm/MAINTAINER.md`). So an input goes in together with a predictor good enough to feed it, and the check is `play: action (scrimmage)`, end to end, not the event's own row.
- A training table is cached per season and never rebuilt on its own: delete `.cache/training/<name>_<season>.parquet` after changing an `inputs.py` or an `example`, or after a season in progress has new games. Building costs a pass over every real play's context, whatever the number of tables: about a minute and 1.5 GB per season and process, so every table of every season takes about 3 minutes on 6 cores (`NFL_TRAINING_WORKERS=3` for fewer processes on a machine short of memory), and all the models another 10.
- Who is playing (`strength.py`): an input about a team or a player is a rate from his past (`Game.form`, `Player.stats`, `Player.career`), one for this season before the game and one for the earlier seasons, and the model weighs them. What was learned adding them, on seasons before the evaluated ones (2019 and 2020 with models trained on 2011 to 2018, then 2013 to 2020 with each season scored by a model of the others):
  - Few, and read coarsely (`fit(..., coarse=WHO)`, `shared/models/README.md`). A team's rate is the same number on every play of its season, so a model reading it exactly, or reading many such numbers at once, can tell the team-seasons in its training plays apart and learns what each went on to do. The score per play doesn't show it; the team's total over a game does. With 60 numbers about the offense the run-or-pass model's dropbacks per team and game were 14% further off than without any, at the same score per play; the quarterback's completion and interception rates made the pass result model's completions per game 3% worse read exactly and 1.5% better read in 8 steps. The exceptions are inputs strong enough to be worth their exact value: the team's shotgun rate and a receiver's depth of target.
  - Measure a team's total over a game, not just the play. An input about who is playing moves every play of a game the same way by a little: a 2% change in the chance of a sack is invisible on one dropback and is most of what separates two quarterbacks over a game. The measure used: per team and game, the model's summed probabilities against what happened, squared.
  - The offense carries it. The quarterback's, the carrier's, the receiver's, and the offense's own past help; the defense's past rates added nothing to sacks, run yards, air yards, or yards after the catch, and little to pass results.
  - Two seasons are too few to choose on, and a score on earlier seasons is not the last word. What 2019 and 2020 suggested was checked on 2013 to 2020 (each season scored by a model of the other seven): the weather on field goals went away; the receiver's catch rate and the weather on pass results, and the weather and the punter's average on punts, appeared. Then the game evaluator, on the evaluated seasons, said no to all four (next point, and `pass_result/predictors/gbm/MAINTAINER.md`).
  - An effect in one event is not an effect on the game. The trained punt model (`punt_kick/predictors/gbm/`) reads the weather and is 2% better punt by punt, and whole games score worse with it: it is the only thing in the simulator that knows the wind, so a windy game gets shorter punts, a shorter field, and more points, when real wind costs points through everything the simulator doesn't tell about it. An input that touches the whole game (the weather, the roof, the surface, the altitude) has to go into the events that carry most of its effect, or into none.
- What each input that went in is worth on the evaluated seasons (every play of 2021 to 2026, each season scored by a model of the seasons before it, with and without the input; per play, and per team and game):

  | event | input | per play | per team and game |
  |---|---|---|---|
  | offensive_formation | the team's shotgun rate | -7.8% | -51% |
  | pass_rush | the quarterback's sacks and scrambles per dropback, his dropbacks, his years in the league | -0.8% | -17% |
  | target | the receiver's air yards per target, his targets | -3.4% | -5.7% |
  | lineups | the team's fourth downs gone for | -1.4% | -3.4% |
  | carry (catch) | the receiver's yards after the catch per reception | -0.4% | -2.3% |
  | pass_result | the quarterback's and the defense's completions and interceptions per attempt | -0.1% | -1.5% |
  | carry (run) | the carrier's yards per rush, his team's per designed run | -0.3% | -1.2% |

  All are beyond 3 standard errors on one of the two measures except the last two per team and game (1.5 and 1.2).
- What it came to in whole games (2026-11-16, 480 games of 2021 to 2026 at 50 sims, case by case against the system before these predictors; two runs of one system differ by about 1% on these rows from simulating alone): `game: margin` 7.929 -> 7.651 (-3.5%, 3.1 standard errors; skill -0.7% -> +2.8%), `game: total` 7.867 -> 7.607 (-3.3%, 2.8), `game: home win` 0.2501 -> 0.2388 (-4.5%, 2.3; skill -0.1% -> +4.4%), `team: points` -3.0% (3.7). Against the betting lines: the moneyline's Brier 0.254 -> 0.243 (the market's own 0.217), the spread's mean absolute error 11.39 -> 10.90 (10.02), the total's 11.17 -> 10.83 (10.67). The player rows that moved: the first quarterback's rushing yards -3.6% (3.3), the first receiver's yards -2.3% (4.3), the first tight end's -2.1% (2.6), and the first back's catches +2.7% worse (3.4: the simulator throws to him too often, 3.0 catches a game against a real 2.6, and now completes those at the rate real ones are; who is thrown to is still the baseline's).
- How much of that is who is playing (2026-11-15, the evaluators' default 160 games at 100 sims, the trained predictors against their blind twins: the same models trained with those inputs blanked out): `game: margin` 7.653 -> 7.363 (-3.8%, 2.4 standard errors), `game: home win` 0.2490 -> 0.2346 (-5.8%, 2.2), `team: points` -2.1% (1.9), `team: pass_yds` -3.8% (2.6), the first quarterback's completions -3.5% (2.3) and rushing yards -16% (6.5). The blind twins were no better than the system before at the margin or the winner (7.653 against 7.585, 0.2490 against 0.2485): what the models learned about situations made plays better (`play: points for minus against (scrimmage)` -3.5%, `lineups: unit (4th down)` -41%), and what they were told about the teams is what moved who wins.
- Every player's every stat (2026-11-16, the evaluators' default 160 games of 2021 to 2026 at 100 sims, case by case; the game evaluator scores them all since then, 584 rows instead of 47):
  - The play scenario credits everything a box score counts and what a charter adds (`scenarios/play/stats.py`). Crediting alone changes no simulated play: every row scored before came out the same to the last digit.
  - Counted instead of hand-set (whether a carry credits a tackler and whether the tackle is shared, by the kind of carry and how it ended; who makes it, who defends a pass, who is flagged, by position group): a team's solo tackles 33.9 -> 40.1 a game against a real 40.7 (with the sack, which a box score also counts as a tackle; `team: tackles` -33%, 9.9 standard errors), a starting defensive back's tackles 2.41 -> 3.19 (real 3.09) and assists 1.96 -> 1.39 (1.44), a starting lineman's passes defended 0.016 -> 0.099 (0.097).
  - Each player's own past in the pick (`shared/priors/MAINTAINER.md` has the rows): starting linebackers' tackles, sacks, and hits 6% to 7% better, linemen's sacks 4%, and the pooled skill of every defender's tackles +0.7% -> +3.5%, assists -2.5% -> +4.1%, sacks -1.3% -> +2.0%, passes defended +0.7% -> +2.9%. It moves who gets a stat, not how many there are, so the game rows don't move (`game: margin` 7.153 -> 7.149).
  - The charted stats come out at the charted rates (a team's pressures 8.47 a game against 8.49, missed tackles 5.67 against 5.70, bad throws 4.99 against 4.98, what was allowed in coverage within 1% of the passing game), and their skill is mostly who is on the field: how often a defender is thrown at +9.8%, pressures +2.1%, missed tackles +1.9%. Blitzes run 10% high (the rate has fallen every season, and the priors still weigh the earlier ones).
  - Still off, and each a predictor's to fix, not the crediting's: flags (4.83 a team against 5.53, the same gap as before, with 37.6 penalty yards against 47.0), tackles for loss (4.94 against 4.28: runs lose yards a little too often, 9.7% of them against 8.8%), passes defended (3.76 against 4.37), fumble recoveries (1.33 against 1.07) and their return yards (hand-set), and a first back's catches (3.06 against 2.52).
- Tried and left out, for no gain per play or per game: the surface, a neutral site, and the draft pick of the player with the ball, anywhere; the weather on field goals, extra points, the shotgun, run or pass, sacks and scrambles, air yards, yards after the catch, run yards, fumbles, and the fourth-down decision (on field goals it looked worth 1.7% per game on 2019 and 2020 and was worth nothing over 2013 to 2020; on the shotgun it and the surface scored until the team's own rate was in, being the home team's by another name), and on pass results and punts, where it scored on earlier seasons and not in whole games (above); the receiver's catch rate on pass results (the same); a kicker's own field goal rate; the defense's sack, scramble, yards per run, yards after the catch, and interception rates; the head coach's own past next to his team's, on the shotgun, run or pass, and the fourth-down decision (the same information: `Game.form(coach)` only differs from the team's after a coaching change); the offense's and the defense's points per game, fumble rates, and flag rates; the team's pass rate on neutral downs in the run-or-pass model, which passed on 2019 and 2020 and was worth nothing on the evaluated seasons (`snap/predictors/gbm/MAINTAINER.md`). An exact draft pick did score: it names the player, which his own past says better. And a trained choice of who is thrown to (`target/predictors/gbm_receiver/`): no receiver's targets, catches, or yards scored better than with the baseline's choice, which already reads his targets per snap.
- Coaches instead of teams (2026-11-16). The question: is a team's past a stand-in for its coaches' and players', and does a system that reads only theirs do better? Every team input a model reads (the shotgun rate, the fourth downs gone for, the yards per designed run, the completions and interceptions allowed) was swapped for the same rate of the coach who decides it (`game.form(...)` of the offensive play caller, the head coach, the defensive play caller), on every real play, with the production settings, in two periods: 2013 to 2020 (each season scored by a model of the others) and 2021 to 2026 (each by a model of the seasons before). Changes are against the team's input, per play and per team and game, with standard errors by game. None was meaningfully better, so the models read what they read before:
  - Shotgun: the play caller's rate instead of the team's, 0.0% and -0.2% on 2013 to 2020, +0.7% and +6.8% worse on 2021 to 2026 (3.6 standard errors); in a new caller's first six weeks +7% and +30% per team and game (1.1 and 3.8). Both together: no better than the team's alone. A new caller keeps the team's quarterback, and the team's rate was standing in for him: the rate of the quarterback's offenses (in the games he was its main passer) with the play caller's was -3.7% per team and game (2.3) and -2.3% (1.4), the best of anything tried and short of the 3 standard errors the inputs in use cleared. It would need the quarterback's offenses as a form, which the inputs don't have.
  - Fourth downs: the head coach's rate instead of the team's, +0.8% and +0.3% per team and game (1.5 and 0.4 standard errors); the play caller's the same. In a new head coach's first season the team's was better (+2.7%, 1.4): 110 of the 119 new head coaches hadn't been one the season before, so there was little or nothing of theirs to read.
  - Run or pass, which reads nobody: the team's, the head coach's, or the play caller's pass rate each made a team's dropbacks per game 1.6% to 4.5% worse (2.4 to 5.9 standard errors), as the team's had before.
  - Pass results: the defensive play caller's completions and interceptions allowed instead of the team's, -0.2% and +0.2% per team and game (0.9 each). Sacks: his sacks per dropback added to the quarterback's, -0.1% and -0.4% (0.4 and 2.4). Run yards, by the mean: the play caller's yards per run instead of the team's, +0.5% and -0.3% (1.5 and 0.7).
  - Why, from the coaches who changed teams (a play caller for eight games or more in two seasons running, at different teams: 59 on offense, 47 on defense; this season's rate against the league's, set against the team's last season and the coach's last season elsewhere). How a unit lines up follows the coach: the shotgun rate correlates 0.51 with his last team's and 0.10 with his new team's own (0.80 where the caller stayed), and the completion rate a defense allows 0.46 with its new caller's last defense and 0.21 with its own. How well a unit plays stays with the team: yards per dropback 0.27 with the team's last season and -0.06 with the caller's, completion rate 0.41 and -0.25, points 0.29 and -0.15, yards per run 0.20 and -0.17. The pass rate follows neither (-0.09, 0.04). Only nine head coaches went straight from one team to another, too few to say anything about fourth downs.
  - So the team is mostly a stand-in for its players, and a coach's own past says something only about scheme, and only for the quarter of new play callers who called plays somewhere the season before: a team starts a season with a new offensive play caller 43% of the time, a new defensive one 38%, and a new head coach 23%. That is too few coach-seasons for a model to learn from, and the efficiency inputs that matter (the quarterback's, the carrier's, the receiver's) are players' already.
  - Not tried: a model that splits a rate between the people who share it (each coach's and player's own effect, estimated together, instead of each one's raw rate), which is what could credit a caller apart from his quarterback; and the coordinators who don't call plays, who are in the inputs by name only.
- Small and not yet used: the referee's flags per snap, worth 1% on a crew's flags per game over 2013 to 2020 (3.6 standard errors) and nothing per play. The referee is in the game's inputs with his form (`game.form(game.referee)`); using it takes a trained fouls predictor, which doesn't exist.
- A number that piles up at a boundary needs the distance to it as an input: the yards models put too little on the goal line until they were told how far each cut is from it (`shared/models/MAINTAINER.md`), and simulated games had the real yards and 80% of the real rushing touchdowns. The per-play scores were better all the same; it showed in the game evaluator's `game: total`, and at once in a table of simulated against real averages, which every score table now has (`mean`, `sim mean`).
- The build's worker processes outlive a killed parent. After interrupting `python -m events.baseline_priors`, kill them too, or they finish their season and write its files.
